Before I begin, I would like to make it clear that I am not an expert book reviewer, nor am I particularly gifted when it comes to reading or analyzing characters. I have read a few books, I enjoy doing it, and I have my own opinions, but that is about it. I am not trying to explain what Jon Fosse meant or find some hidden meaning behind everything that happens. I simply want to talk about what I thought of *Vaim*.

I should also warn you now that there will be spoilers. A lot of them. This is not exactly a summary either, although I will inevitably have to discuss parts of the story to explain what I liked and what bothered me.

The book came to me through a mystery box from Gandhi Bookstores. The idea was to receive a book every month based on my personal tastes, which sounded great to me because one of the things I struggle with the most is deciding what to read next. I liked the idea of someone else making that decision and the possibility of receiving something I would never have bought on my own.

The customer service, on the other hand, was terrible. The questionnaire they used to learn about my preferences also felt far too short, so I was not convinced they could truly understand what kind of reader I am from so few questions. Even so, they may have gotten it right with *Vaim*. It was not a book I disliked and, although it took me about a month to finish because I was reading other things at the same time, it kept me curious enough to continue until the end.

The first thing that stands out about *Vaim* is the way it is written.

Jon Fosse writes as though thoughts are incapable of stopping. There are no paragraph breaks in the way we normally expect them, and almost everything moves through commas, repetitions, dialogue, and thoughts that gradually blend into one another. At times, it is difficult to tell when one person is speaking and when the other person is answering. Sometimes you do not understand who said something until the character clarifies it afterward.

I found it quite difficult at first.

It is not because the book uses difficult words. The language is simple, and you do not need to keep a dictionary nearby. The problem is getting into its rhythm. Whenever I picked it up again, I needed a few minutes of concentration to readjust. When I tried to read while distracted, I could go through several lines without properly understanding who was speaking or what had just happened.

After a while, however, the reading begins to flow.

The book feels fast even when what is happening is extremely slow. You can spend entire pages reading about a character deciding whether to open a door, buying some thread, or walking somewhere, but the way the thoughts are linked together keeps you moving forward.

For me, that is the best part of the novel. It is also practically the only thing that saves it.

The story is divided into three chapters. The first follows Jatgeir, the second Elías, and the third Frank, whose real name is Olav. Although none of the chapters is told from her perspective, Eline is the person who connects the entire story.

I would have liked to know what she was thinking.

Eline makes the decisions that change everyone’s lives, but we never enter her mind. We only know her through what the men think about her. This leaves many unanswered questions, especially because her behavior is quite strange and, at times, she seems capable of making everyone do whatever she wants without having to convince them very much.

The first chapter begins with Jatgeir traveling in his small fishing boat, which is also named Eline. He named it after a woman he knew when he was young and whom, according to what he tries to tell himself, he may not have truly loved, although everything suggests that he never stopped thinking about her.

Jatgeir arrives in the city and goes looking for a needle and some thread because he needs to sew on a button. Starting from something so simple, we spend a great deal of time inside his head as he walks, remembers things, and thinks about the same ideas over and over again.

I saw Jatgeir as someone who was fairly limited intellectually. I am not saying the book explicitly describes him that way, but that was the impression his internal monologue gave me. He gets stuck on very simple thoughts, repeats ideas, and reformulates things we have only just read.

There were moments when I thought my copy had a printing error. I would read an idea and, shortly afterward, almost exactly the same thing would appear again. Then I understood that it was not a mistake in the book. The character was simply thinking about it again.

He is also someone who is incapable of refusing anything.

His real name is Geir, but people call him Jatgeir—Ja-tgeir—because he always says yes. This explains his behavior quite well, since *ja* means “yes” in Norwegian. When he goes to buy the needle and thread, he is charged a ridiculous amount of money. He knows he is being scammed, feels humiliated, and pays anyway. He later buys practically the same things somewhere else and once again pays an absurd amount. By coincidence, it is exactly the same amount he paid before.

He does not complain. He does not defend himself. He does not say no.

The same inability appears when Eline arrives at his boat in the middle of the night.

She appears carrying her luggage and tells him they have to leave. She has left her husband, Frank, and wants to return to Vaim with Jatgeir. He has spent years thinking about her and even named his boat after her, so in theory this should be the moment he had been waiting for.

Their conversation, however, felt very uncomfortable to me.

Fosse’s dialogue was one of the things I enjoyed the least. It is too brief, dry, and sometimes even childish. One character says hello and the other answers. One says that a long time has passed and the other says yes. Sometimes it feels as though neither of them knows how to continue the conversation.

Perhaps that discomfort is intentional. Perhaps that is simply how these people speak. Either way, I did not like it.

It is also difficult to follow the change from one voice to another because there are not always clear indicators. The character thinks, someone speaks, the character responds, and only afterward do you fully understand who said what.

Eline demands that Jatgeir leave quickly because she is afraid Frank will arrive. He obeys. As they leave the harbor, they cross paths with another boat, the *Elinor*, carrying three fishermen. We later learn that they were Frank and his two friends.

Once Eline is actually with him, Jatgeir begins to wonder whether he truly wants to be by her side. This was one of the most interesting parts of his chapter. He had spent years longing for her, remembering her, and using her name, but once she is standing in front of him, he is no longer sure.

Perhaps he was in love with the memory rather than the person.

The second chapter was my favorite.

Here we follow Elías, Jatgeir’s best friend. At first, I felt as though I were reading exactly the same character again. His internal monologue sounded very similar, and Elías also repeated his thoughts far too often. At that point, I believed Fosse simply wrote every character’s mind in the same way.

Later, when I reached Frank’s chapter, I changed my mind somewhat because Frank does think differently. His thoughts are much more organized. Because of that, I ended up believing that the resemblance between Elías and Jatgeir was part of how both characters were constructed.

Elías lives alone and comes across as an extremely lonely person. In one scene, someone begins knocking on the door of his house. He does not want to open it because the house is untidy and he does not want to let anyone inside. He then begins arguing with himself about whether he should get up or remain where he is.

He can spend an entire page thinking about it.

Eventually, he opens the door and no one is there.

He goes back inside, someone knocks again, he opens the door, and still no one is there. At first he becomes annoyed, then frightened. He starts thinking that he needs to go to the market or to church, even though he is not particularly religious. In reality, all he wants is to be surrounded by other people because he is afraid of being alone.

After several knocks, he opens the door once more and finds Jatgeir standing there.

They had not seen each other for a long time. Part of the distance between them came from the fact that Elías did not approve of his friend living with a married woman. Even so, Jatgeir had been his best friend for many years, and his appearance feels strange, especially because he arrives, says very little, and leaves almost immediately.

Before leaving, he mentions that he saw a man near the house, someone who seemed like a ghost because he disappeared.

Elías is still frightened and decides to go to the market. There, he finds several fishermen with downcast faces. He speaks to them and learns that Jatgeir has died. His body was found floating face-up in the water.

We never find out exactly what happened to him.

That bothered me quite a lot. I wanted to know how he died, whether it was an accident, whether he fell from the boat, or whether something else happened. Fosse does not spend much time explaining it. He focuses on Elías, who is trying to understand how his friend can be dead when he has just seen him standing outside his door.

Then everything that happened before begins to take on a different meaning.

Perhaps Jatgeir was the one knocking. Perhaps he was already dead when he finally entered the house. Perhaps he had come to say goodbye. The book changes tone, and for a moment you no longer know whether you are reading a love story, a ghost story, or simply the confusion of a man who has just received terrible news.

Elías returns home and feels that Jatgeir is in the sky. He raises his hand to say goodbye and feels his friend returning the gesture.

It was my favorite moment in the book.

It is not a huge or particularly elaborate scene, but I found it beautiful. Elías, who spent the whole chapter frightened and alone, is finally able to say goodbye to his best friend. That scene made me enjoy the second chapter far more than the other two.

Then Frank’s part begins.

Here we learn what happened from the perspective of Eline’s husband, and we also discover that Frank is not actually named Frank. His real name is Olav.

When Eline meets him, she begins calling him Frank, and he never manages to tell her that it is not his name. During his internal monologue, he repeatedly reminds himself that his name is Olav, simply Olav, as though it were something he could only assert inside his own head.

Eline is not really called Eline either. Her name is Josephine. Jatgeir was Geir, and Frank was Olav. All three people involved in the love triangle were using names other than the ones they were given at birth.

I do not know whether Fosse intended to say something specific with this. It caught my attention, but I also do not want to invent an explanation for him. What is clear is that Eline has an enormous influence on the way the others live, even on the names they use.

Frank tells us how he met her. She approached him and his companions, apparently drunk, and began deciding what they should do. She told them they should all leave together on the boat. Frank did not know why he was obeying her, but he did. His friends did the same.

Then Eline decided that she and Frank would make a good couple and that they should get married.

Once again, he agreed.

Their relationship also seemed strange to me, which fits with Frank eventually saying that his life was “simply strange.” It does not feel like a great love story. It seems more as though Eline makes a decision and Frank allows himself to be carried along because he does not have the will to refuse.

Years later, Eline leaves him a letter and abandons him to go away with Jatgeir.

Frank does not react with much drama either. He does not seem devastated, although he is not completely relieved. He admits that he himself was not sure he wanted to remain with her.

The next day, he buys his own fishing boat, something he had wanted to do for a long time. He calls it Eline, a decision he had already made before she left.

That is how there come to be two boats with the same name: Jatgeir’s and Frank’s.

When Jatgeir dies, Eline inherits his belongings because he had no close family. Months later, she returns for Frank. She appears with the same urgency as always, tells him to pack his things, and asks him to go with her to Vaim.

And once again, he agrees.

The situation seems very strange to me. Your wife leaves you to be with the love of her youth, the man dies, and a few months later she returns to take you to live in the house that belonged to that same man.

Frank does not put up much resistance. He goes with her and ends up living among Jatgeir’s belongings and the two boats named Eline.

As the years pass, Elías also dies and is buried next to Jatgeir.

Eline asks Frank to have her buried as close as possible to Jatgeir when she dies. She even suggests removing Elías’s body so that the place next to him will be available.

I do not know how anyone is supposed to receive a request like that. Your wife leaves you for someone else, returns when that person dies, and then asks you to bury her beside him. Frank seems to accept things with a calmness I cannot understand.

In the end, Elías is not exhumed. When Eline dies, she is buried next to Elías, which at least fulfills her wish to be buried close to Jatgeir.

Her gravestone bears the name Josephine, her real name. Almost no one knows that the person buried there is Eline. Frank, or rather Olav, is the one who knows the full story.

By then, everyone except him has died.

Olav is left with the house, the belongings, and the boats. He sells the one that belonged to Jatgeir and keeps his own. Now an old man, he thinks that when he dies, he will leave everything to the Church or to charity.

His chapter ends with that insistence on remembering that he is Olav. Simply Olav, as he says that he wants a cross on his gravestone with his name written on it.

When I closed the book, I thought that all the characters were fairly boring. Not only because the story itself can become boring, which it sometimes does, but because their lives seem empty beyond work, boats, and this love triangle.

Jatgeir works and lives on his boat. Elías is alone in his house. Frank works as a fisherman and accepts almost anything Eline decides. None of them seem to have many interests or ambitions.

Eline is the only one who moves from place to place, abandons people, returns, gives orders, and changes the lives of everyone around her. Even so, because we never know her thoughts, we do not know what she was truly looking for. I found the story very simple.

I believe that if it had been written in a conventional way, with normal paragraphs, traditional punctuation, and clearer dialogue, it would have been a very bad novel or, at the very least, a completely forgettable one. There is not much about the love triangle that I found original. The characters are passive, the dialogue is uncomfortable, and very little of importance happens during more than half of the book. You can read around a hundred pages before reaching Jatgeir’s death. That was the point when the novel truly began to interest me.

I kept reading mainly because of the way Fosse writes. There is something about his repetitions and that apparently endless flow of thought that pushes you to continue, even while it can also become frustrating. I do not think he is a genius, at least not after reading only this book. I do think he has a very distinctive way of writing. That does not mean I will enjoy everything he does with that style.

I would read another book by Jon Fosse, but the story would have to interest me a great deal. I do not know whether I would be willing to put up with another plot this simple just to experience his writing style again.

I give *Vaim* a 7 out of 10.

Not because I thought its story was good, but because, despite all my complaints, I finished the book and I still find myself thinking about some of its scenes. Mainly Elías raising his hand toward the sky to say goodbye to Jatgeir.
